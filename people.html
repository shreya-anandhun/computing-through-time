The people

Machines get the headlines, but two people in particular decided what a computer would be. One imagined it on paper; the other made it understand us.

1912 – 1954 · England

Alan Turing

In 1936, aged 23, Turing wrote a paper describing an imaginary machine that could follow any set of instructions written on a strip of tape. That “universal machine” is the blueprint for every computer you have used: one device, any program. He described it a decade before anyone could build one.

During the Second World War he worked at Bletchley Park, Britain’s code-breaking centre, where he helped design the Bombe, a machine that cracked the German Enigma cipher and is credited with shortening the war. In 1950 he asked the question “Can machines think?” and proposed a test for it, now called the Turing test, which people still argue about every time a chatbot gets good.

Britain prosecuted him in 1952 for being gay, and he died two years later. He was formally pardoned in 2013, and since 2021 his face has been on the £50 note.

  • 1
    1936: describes the universal machine, the idea behind all computers.
  • 2
    1939–45: helps break Enigma at Bletchley Park.
  • 3
    1950: proposes the Turing test for machine intelligence.
“I propose to consider the question, ‘Can machines think?’” Opening line of his 1950 paper, Computing Machinery and Intelligence

1906 – 1992 · United States

Grace Hopper

Grace Hopper was a maths professor who joined the US Navy during the Second World War and was sent to program the Harvard Mark I, one of the first computers in the world. Programming it meant writing raw machine code, and she thought that was absurd: computers should learn our language, not the other way round.

So in 1952 she built the first compiler, a program that turned English-like commands into instructions the machine could run. Almost nobody believed it would work. That idea grew into COBOL in 1959, a language that still quietly runs banks and airlines today, and into every programming language you will learn at college.

She retired from the Navy as a rear admiral at 79, the oldest serving officer at the time, and was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016.

  • 1
    1944: one of the first programmers of the Harvard Mark I.
  • 2
    1947: her team finds a real moth in a computer and logs the first literal bug.
  • 3
    1952: writes the first compiler, so people can code in words.
“The most dangerous phrase in the language is, ‘We’ve always done it this way.’” Grace Hopper, in interviews throughout the 1980s
